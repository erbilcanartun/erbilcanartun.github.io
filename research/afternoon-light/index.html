Could the color of afternoon light be a cue for sundowning?

Afternoon daylight can still look bright as its color shifts towards red. Could this change help explain why some people with dementia become unsettled late in the day, a pattern known as sundowning?

We modeled the spectrum of daylight reaching the eye at three European latitudes. We measured its color using a scale weighted for melanopsin, a light-sensitive protein involved in setting the body clock. Per unit of visual brightness, that measure fell by about a quarter between solar noon and a solar altitude of ten degrees in our clear-sky calculation, while total brightness changed by only 3 to 10 percent. The light changes color well before it grows dark.

The calculation suggests a way to test the idea. When clocks change for daylight saving time, does symptom onset follow the Sun or keep to the same clock hour? The study models light exposure. Testing whether it contributes to symptoms requires observations in people.
